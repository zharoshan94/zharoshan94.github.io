Extratropical moist heat extremes

In my postdoctoral research, I have focused on a key question with major societal relevance: What sets the upper limit for moist heat extremes in the extratropical region? Moist heat extremes are closely related to near-surface moist static energy which is coupled to free-tropospheric saturation moist static energy through moist convection. Using reanalysis data and global climate-model simulations, I showed that entrainment of dry air from the lower free troposphere can suppress convection while increasing boundary-layer instability, allowing near-surface heat and humidity to accumulate. The framework links extreme moist heat to changes in free-tropospheric moist static energy, lower-tropospheric saturation deficit, and entrainment strength under warming. This work provides a physically grounded basis for diagnosing and projecting changes in moist heat extremes outside the tropics.

Publications

Atmospheric dynamics and land-atmosphere interactions

My Ph.D. research has advanced understanding of the coupled atmospheric and land-surface processes that control pre-monsoon heat extremes over South Asia. I investigated these processes across interannual, sub-seasonal, and event timescales, linking changes in large-scale circulation to the persistence and intensification of pre-monsoon heat extremes in India.

At the interannual scale, I showed that the recent increase in heatwave persistence over North Central India is linked to a northward shift and weakening of mid-tropospheric zonal winds that promote persistent anticyclonic circulations, with anthropogenic greenhouse-gas forcing contributing to these dynamical changes. At sub-seasonal scales, I identified an emerging 20-60 day temperature mode associated with the Madden-Julian Oscillation, whose inadequate representation in forecast models limits heatwave predictability beyond 10-12 days. At the event scale, I demonstrated that the March and April 2022 South Asian heatwaves were driven, respectively, by anomalous wave activity and soil moisture depletion, showing how atmospheric anomalies can initiate extreme heat which is then sustained and intensified by soil moisture deficits. Together, this work advances scientific understanding of how changes in large-scale atmospheric circulation and land-atmosphere feedbacks interact across timescales to produce heat extremes.

Land surface modelling (Irrigation module)

My Ph.D. research has advanced the representation and evaluation of human water management in land surface models. I examined how irrigation is represented in the Community Land Model (CLM), which is widely used as the land component of Earth system models, and assessed the consequences of its assumptions for simulating land-atmosphere interactions. Irrigation is an important climate adaptation for food production and heat alleviation, but its effects can only be assessed reliably when models accurately represent where, when, and how much water is applied. I focused on the Indo-Gangetic Plain, one of the world's most intensively cultivated and densely populated regions, where irrigation has often been hypothesised to substantially reduce heat stress through enhanced evapotranspiration and surface cooling. I combined observed irrigation information with regional climate-model simulations coupled to CLM to test this hypothesis. This framework allowed me to distinguish the effect of observed agricultural water use from that produced by the model's internally prescribed irrigation scheme. I demonstrated that simplified irrigation parameterisations often based on idealised soil-moisture thresholds, prescribed crop calendars, and assumptions of readily available water can produce misleading estimates of the hydroclimatic benefits and risks of irrigation.

Minor Projects

Attribution of extreme precipitation and flooding in Kathmandu Valley

This project examines the September 2024 flooding disaster in Kathmandu Valley, where exceptional rainfall combined with rapid urbanisation and landscape change to produce severe impacts. The work links the event to a synoptic-scale low-pressure system over northern India and a secondary cyclonic vortex over western India, which together strengthened moisture transport from the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal towards the Himalayas. Using rapid attribution methods with observations and high-resolution climate models, the study evaluates how human-induced climate change altered the likelihood and intensity of the rainfall event, while also highlighting how exposure and urban growth amplified the damage on the ground.
